On day six, you stop looking at strangers. You take the train home. You open the front door of your childhood house. The smell of cinnamon and roasted tea. Your mother turns from the stove with a warm smile. Your father looks up from his crossword puzzle. And before you can stop yourself, your eyes flick upward.

Mother: seven years, four months. Father: three years, eight months. A physical blow strikes your chest. You've always known intellectually that your parents wouldn't live forever. Every human knows this. Yet we postpone visits. We answer their phone calls with rushed, distracted voices. We tell ourselves: I'll visit next weekend. I'll take them to dinner next month. When work calms down.

Standing in that kitchen, looking at three years and eight months, the arithmetic of your entire life rewrites itself. If you visit twice a year, three years means you will see your father six more times. Not hundreds. Six. For the rest of the evening, you don't check your phone once. You ask your father about his childhood in the countryside. You listen to every single detail of your mother's recipe. You hold their hands when you leave. And for the first time in your life, you aren't living in the phantom future. You are fully, acutely, painfully alive in the present.
