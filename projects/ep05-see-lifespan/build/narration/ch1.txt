You wake up at midnight, look in the mirror, and the voices in your head are gone. Instead, ticking silently on your forehead is a luminous digital clock. Fifty-two years. One hundred and eighty-four days. Fourteen hours. And counting down with every heartbeat. You touch your brow. Cold skin. But the digits remain. You step out onto your balcony. A street cleaner is sweeping autumn leaves. Above his head: four years, two months. A teenager laughs on a skateboard: sixty-eight years. And then you see a young, athletic jogger in neon running shoes. His clock is glowing dark red: Zero years. Zero days. One hour and fourteen minutes. For the next seven days, you can see the exact expiration date of every human soul on earth. And by day seven, you will realize that humans don't fear dying. We fear realizing how much of life we threw away while pretending we were immortal.

At first, your instinct is sheer panic. Because you realize that every room you enter is filled with people walking toward invisible trapdoors. At the subway station, hundreds of numbers bob in unison. Thirty years. Fifty years. Twelve years. And you notice something bizarre: The people with fifty or sixty years on their clocks are the ones looking miserable. They sit with slouched shoulders, doom-scrolling short videos, staring into screens with vacant eyes. They take out their phones, ignore their children, and snap at the ticket vendor. Why? Because when your clock says you have fifty years left, each individual hour feels essentially worthless. You treat time like Monopoly money because you assume the bank never closes. Roman philosopher Seneca wrote about this two thousand years ago: You live as if you are destined to live forever. No thought of your frailty ever enters your head. When death feels distant, life feels cheap.
