On day two, you can't stop thinking about the jogger. The neon shoes. The one hour and fourteen minutes. You check local news. At one fifteen yesterday morning, a delivery truck ran a light three blocks away. A jogger was struck. The clock wasn't a probability. It wasn't a warning. It was a fact.

By day three, the knowledge becomes an unbearable weight. You walk into your office. Kevin's clock says forty-one years. Your manager's clock says twenty-eight years. Then an elderly courier delivers a package to the reception desk. His timer: two days, three hours. He is laughing, telling a joke to the receptionist, entirely unaware that forty-eight hours from now, his story on earth is finished. You freeze. Do you tell him? Excuse me, sir. Go home. Call your daughter. Cancel your shifts. What would he say? He'd think you're insane. He'd call building security.

Psychologists call this Terror Management Theory: humans maintain elaborate cultural systems and daily routines specifically to suppress the terrifying awareness of our mortality. If you tear away that veil without warning, you don't liberate someone. You induce psychological collapse. You realize the horror of omniscience: knowing the future doesn't give you control over it. It only forces you to grieve before the loss happens.
