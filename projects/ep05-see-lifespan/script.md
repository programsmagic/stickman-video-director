# EP05 — What If You Could See Everyone's Lifespan?

**Series:** "What If You Could See Everyone's ___?" on @programsmagic. Mascot: **Magi** (wizard hat, glasses, yellow scarf).
**Style:** B1 code-drawn · 16:9 · English · Gemini Bodi narration (native, 1×)
**Length:** ~1,400 spoken words ≈ 8 min runtime.
**Status:** DRAFT — written with deep psychological framing and ready for narration synthesis.

## Psychological Architecture & Real-World Framing
- **The Core Paradox:** Humans are the only animals capable of contemplating their own mortality, yet we survive psychologically by pretending death is a distant theoretical concept (Terror Management Theory).
- **The Illusion of "Someday":** As Stoic philosopher Seneca observed 2,000 years ago in *De Brevitate Vitae*, humans guard their money with paranoia, but recklessly squander the only asset they can never recover: time.
- **The Emotional Arc:**
  1. *The Shock (Day 1):* The visual horror of seeing digital countdown clocks ticking on every forehead.
  2. *The Distortion (Day 2–3):* The illusion of safety. Long-clock people waste life in endless procrastination; short-clock people illuminate the present with terrifying clarity.
  3. *The Intervention Trap (Day 4–5):* What happens when you try to save someone whose clock reads zero? The psychological burden of playing god.
  4. *The Parent's Clock (Day 6):* Visiting your aging parents, looking at their numbers, and confronting the unspoken arithmetic of family love.
  5. *The Liberation (Day 7):* Understanding that mortality is not an enemy to outsmart; it is the boundary that gives every touch, conversation, and sunset infinite value.

## Script

### 1 · HOOK (0:00–0:35)
**[Midnight. The whispers fade into absolute silence. Magi looks into the bathroom mirror. Glowing above his brow, pulsing in soft cyan light, is a digital timer: "52Y : 184D : 14H : 22M : 18S". The seconds tick down steadily.]**
You wake up at midnight, look in the mirror, and the voices in your head are gone.
Instead, ticking silently on your forehead is a luminous digital clock.
Fifty-two years. One hundred and eighty-four days. Fourteen hours.
And counting down with every heartbeat.
You touch your brow. Cold skin. But the digits remain.
You step out onto your balcony.
A street cleaner is sweeping autumn leaves. Above his head: four years, two months.
A teenager laughs on a skateboard: sixty-eight years.
And then you see a young, athletic jogger in neon running shoes.
His clock is glowing dark red:
Zero years. Zero days. One hour and fourteen minutes.
For the next seven days, you can see the exact expiration date of every human soul on earth.
And by day seven, you will realize that humans don't fear dying.
We fear realizing how much of life we threw away while pretending we were immortal.
**[Scene text: "7 DAYS. EVERY EXPIRATION DATE."]**

### 2 · DAY 1 — THE ILLUSION OF SAFETY
At first, your instinct is sheer panic.
Because you realize that every room you enter is filled with people walking toward invisible trapdoors.
At the subway station, hundreds of numbers bob in unison.
Thirty years. Fifty years. Twelve years.
And you notice something bizarre:
The people with fifty or sixty years on their clocks are the ones looking miserable.
They sit with slouched shoulders, doom-scrolling short videos, staring into screens with vacant eyes.
They take out their phones, ignore their children, and snap at the ticket vendor.
Why? Because when your clock says you have fifty years left, each individual hour feels essentially worthless.
You treat time like Monopoly money because you assume the bank never closes.
Roman philosopher Seneca wrote about this two thousand years ago:
"You live as if you are destined to live forever. No thought of your frailty ever enters your head."
When death feels distant, life feels cheap.
**[Scene text: "WHEN TIME FEELS INFINITE, IT FEELS WORTHLESS."]**

### 3 · DAY 2 & 3 — THE JOGGER AND THE BURDEN
On day two, you can't stop thinking about the jogger.
The neon shoes. The one hour and fourteen minutes.
You check local news.
At 1:15 AM yesterday morning, a delivery truck ran a light three blocks away. A jogger was struck.
The clock wasn't a probability. It wasn't a warning.
It was a fact.
By day three, the knowledge becomes an unbearable weight.
You walk into your office.
Kevin's clock says forty-one years. Your manager's clock says twenty-eight years.
Then an elderly courier delivers a package to the reception desk.
His timer: two days, three hours.
He is laughing, telling a joke to the receptionist, entirely unaware that forty-eight hours from now, his story on earth is finished.
You freeze. Do you tell him?
"Excuse me, sir. Go home. Call your daughter. Cancel your shifts."
What would he say? He'd think you're insane. He'd call building security.
Psychologists call this Terror Management Theory: humans maintain elaborate cultural systems and daily routines specifically to suppress the terrifying awareness of our mortality.
If you tear away that veil without warning, you don't liberate someone. You induce psychological collapse.
You realize the horror of omniscience: knowing the future doesn't give you control over it. It only forces you to grieve before the loss happens.
**[Scene text: "KNOWLEDGE WITHOUT POWER IS JUST PREMATURE GRIEF."]**

### 4 · DAY 4 & 5 — THE INTERVENTION
On day four, you refuse to be passive.
You decide you're going to break the rule.
At the city library, a college student is studying alone at a wooden desk.
Her timer: thirty-five minutes.
You sit two tables away, watching the seconds melt.
At ten minutes left, she packs her laptop into her canvas bag, stands up, and walks toward the exit.
You follow her.
She steps onto the crosswalk. A city bus turns the corner fast, blinding rain on the windshield.
You don't think. You sprint forward, grab her backpack, and yank her backward onto the sidewalk.
The bus roars past, spraying puddle water across her jeans.
She stumbles, gasping, furious and terrified:
"What is wrong with you?! Let go of me!"
You look at her forehead.
The timer didn't reset to eighty years.
It didn't disappear.
The digits simply shifted by sixteen minutes:
Zero hours. Sixteen minutes. Twenty-eight seconds.
Your stomach drops into ice water.
You didn't cure mortality. You merely postponed the collision.
Death wasn't a specific bus on a specific street. It was an inevitable biological and physical certainty woven into the fabric of time.
By day five, after shadowing her and watching her collapse from an undiagnosed aneurysm in a pharmacy, you understand:
You cannot cheat the ledger. You can only torture yourself trying to outrun it.
**[Scene text: "YOU CANNOT OUTSMART THE LEDGER."]**

### 5 · DAY 6 — THE PARENTS' KITCHEN
On day six, you stop looking at strangers.
You take the train home.
You open the front door of your childhood house. The smell of cinnamon and roasted tea.
Your mother turns from the stove with a warm smile. Your father looks up from his crossword puzzle.
And before you can stop yourself, your eyes flick upward.
Mother: seven years, four months.
Father: three years, eight months.
A physical blow strikes your chest.
You've always known intellectually that your parents wouldn't live forever.
Every human knows this.
Yet we postpone visits. We answer their phone calls with rushed, distracted voices.
We tell ourselves: "I'll visit next weekend. I'll take them to dinner next month. When work calms down."
Standing in that kitchen, looking at "three years, eight months," the arithmetic of your entire life rewrites itself.
If you visit twice a year, three years means you will see your father six more times.
Not hundreds. Six.
For the rest of the evening, you don't check your phone once.
You ask your father about his childhood in the countryside. You listen to every single detail of your mother's recipe.
You hold their hands when you leave.
And for the first time in your life, you aren't living in the phantom future.
You are fully, acutely, painfully alive in the present.
**[Scene text: "THE PRESENT IS THE ONLY PLACE LOVE CAN LIVE."]**

### 6 · DAY 7 — THE FRAME OF THE PAINTING
Day seven arrives.
Midnight approaches.
You stand before the mirror in your dark apartment.
Your own forehead is ticking down.
Fifty-two years.
You look at the numbers, and the fear is gone.
Instead, a profound reverence fills the room.
If human life were infinite, no decision would ever carry weight.
You could learn guitar in five hundred years. You could apologize in three centuries. You could forgive someone tomorrow, or in a millennium.
Because nothing would ever be lost.
A painting is only beautiful because of the frame that encloses it.
Music is only heartbreaking because the chord must eventually end.
And love only burns with divine intensity because the people we love can be taken from us.
The digital clock reaches 11:59:50.
Ten seconds.
Five.
Four.
Three.
Two.
One.
The glowing blue digits dissolve into thin air.
Your reflection looks back at you.
Smooth brow. No countdown. No spoilers.
You don't know if you have fifty years left, fifty days, or fifty minutes.
And that is the greatest gift the universe ever gave you.
Because the only day that ever matters is the one you are standing in right now.

Tomorrow, the countdown disappears.
Until you meet someone, look into their eyes, and see a word floating above their lips every time they speak.
What if you could see every single lie?
Subscribe. Because tomorrow, the truth comes out.
