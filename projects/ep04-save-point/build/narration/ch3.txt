On day four, reality stops being a game. At two fifteen, you're walking across Fifth Street. A delivery van runs a red light and clips a cyclist. You scream, rush over, and see blood on the pavement. Instinct kicks in. You tap your wrists.

You wake up. You have seven hours to prevent it. At two ten, you stand at that exact intersection. When the cyclist arrives, you step in front of him and ask for directions to delay him by thirty seconds. He stops, points down the street, annoyed. The delivery van speeds past through the intersection. No crash. You breathe a sigh of relief. You saved him.

Until you turn the corner. Because you delayed the cyclist, the delivery van entered the next intersection two seconds earlier than before. Where it slammed directly into a crowded city bus. Four people are injured. You reload.

You wake up at seven. Heart pounding. Sweating. You try again. You pull the fire alarm in the coffee shop to delay the van driver. That causes a traffic jam that blocks an ambulance three miles away. No matter what you tweak, chaotic systems refuse to be tamed. In complex systems, eliminating one point of friction always displaces energy somewhere else. By day five, you haven't slept. You've lived through Tuesday nineteen times. You realize the horrible truth: you aren't playing god. You're just a frantic janitor trying to mop the ocean.
