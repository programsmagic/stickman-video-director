By day two, the games get bigger. You watch the opening bell. You note which stock spikes three hundred percent by noon. Then you reload. At seven in the morning, you put your entire savings into that exact trade. By lunch, you're wealthy. By day three, you've tried thirty-two different ways to handle your morning meeting until you deliver a presentation so flawless your manager gives you a standing ovation. You feel invincible.

Until five o'clock on day three. You're sitting on your couch, looking at your bank account, and something cold creeps into your stomach. You want to order dinner, but suddenly, you hesitate. What if the pizza place is slow tonight? What if the sushi place is better? Normally, you'd just pick one, eat, and move on. Now? You order pizza, eat two slices, tap your wrists, reload, and order sushi. Then you reload again because you wonder if the Thai curry was the optimal choice.

Psychologists have a name for this: Maximization Paralysis. When mistakes are impossible to undo, humans accept good-enough outcomes. When every choice can theoretically be perfected, satisfaction drops to zero. Because now, every minor imperfection is your fault for not resetting.
