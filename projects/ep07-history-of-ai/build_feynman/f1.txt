Look down at your phone. It feels like magic, but inside, it is fundamentally just a handful of sand from the beach.
Sand is made of tiny quartz crystals called silicon.
Humans figured out that if you melt sand down and carve billions of microscopic switches into it, you can trap electricity inside.
When a switch is OFF, it means ZERO. When it flips ON, it means ONE.
That is all a computer is: a beach of sand with lightning flipping switches.
So how on earth did we teach sand to think?
In 1950, a British math genius named Alan Turing published a landmark paper. He asked seven simple words: "Can machines think?"
Turing imagined a machine with an infinite paper tape, like a never-ending comic strip. And he proved that every computer in the universe only needs seven simple rules—what mathematicians call the seven variables:
First, Q: the robot's mood or memory state.
Second, Sigma: the secret alphabet it reads, like zeros and ones.
Third, Gamma: all the symbols written on its paper tape.
Fourth, Delta: the recipe book. If you see a one, erase it, write a zero, and step right!
Fifth, Q-zero: the start button where the robot wakes up.
Sixth, B: an empty blank box on the paper waiting for an idea.
And seventh, F: the finish line, when the puzzle is solved and the robot says: I did it!
Six years later, in 1956, two scientists named John McCarthy and Claude Shannon gathered in an attic room at Dartmouth College. They officially gave this dream a brand-new name: "Artificial Intelligence."
