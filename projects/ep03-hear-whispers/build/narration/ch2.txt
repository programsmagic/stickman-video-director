On day three, you hear the dark side. You drop your pen in the corridor, step around the corner, and listen. One coworker tells another: I heard Magi was ten minutes late yesterday because he got stopped by security. Ten minutes later, you hear that same coworker talking to someone on the fourth floor: Magi had an incident with building security yesterday. Something about his pass being revoked. By three in the afternoon, the whisper reaching your ear is completely unhinged: Did you hear Magi got escorted out by guards? You stand by the elevators, stunned. You weren't late. You stopped to tie your shoelace. How did tying a shoelace turn into armed guards? In 1932, psychologist Frederic Bartlett ran experiments on how stories travel from person to person. He found that rumors mutate through three predictable stages: Leveling, people drop the boring details. Sharpening, people exaggerate the dramatic parts. And assimilation, people twist the story to fit what they already believe. Every time a sentence travels behind your back, it loses truth and gains drama. The whisper wasn't about who you are. It was about human entertainment.

On day four, you hear the sentence you were dreading. You walk out to the car park. Your ears buzz. The whisper clicks on. It hits like ice water down your neck. Your hands grip the steering wheel. Your stomach knots. Exhausting? Overthinks everything? You've known this person for seven years. You helped them move apartments. You listened to their breakups. For three hours, you sit in your car, replaying those eight words on repeat. Psychologists call this the negativity bias. Our ancestors survived by paying ten times more attention to danger than to safety. A predator in the bushes matters more than a ripe berry on a branch. So your brain treats one piece of negative gossip like a life-or-death wound, while completely erasing a hundred kind things. Then you realize something painful. Are you exhausting sometimes? Do you overthink? Yes. You do. A cruel sentence spoken behind your back hurts. But sometimes, buried underneath the sting, it's the only mirror honest enough to tell you what no one will say to your face.
