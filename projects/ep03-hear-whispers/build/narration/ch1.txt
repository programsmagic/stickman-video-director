You wake up, and the glowing numbers are gone. Your head feels quiet. Normal, even. Until you step out of your bedroom, close the front door, and take two steps down the hallway. A voice. Faint, clear, and right in your ear. Your roommate, talking inside the apartment you just left. You freeze. You didn't leave your phone on. You don't have an earpiece in. For the next seven days, whenever you leave a room, you can hear every single sentence spoken about you. Your friends. Your coworkers. Your family. The stranger you bumped into on the subway. And by day seven, the quietest whisper in the city will change how you treat every person in your life.

At first, your heart pounds through your ribcage. Because your entire life, you've wondered: what do people actually say when the door closes? You step outside the coffee shop. You listen. Wait. They're not mocking you. In fact, after twenty seconds, they stop talking about you completely. They're arguing about parking tickets. Lesson one, before breakfast: people talk about you far less than you think. Psychologists call this the spotlight effect. We live inside our own heads all day, so we naturally assume everyone else is thinking about us too. In famous experiments at Cornell, researchers found people overestimate how much others notice them by more than double. Most of the time, the moment you leave the room, you vanish from their minds. And that's not insulting. It's a relief.

Then day two arrives, and you walk into work. At lunch, you stand up from the break room, excuse yourself to wash your hands, and linger outside the door. Your jaw drops. Kevin. The guy who barely says five words in morning meetings. He isn't taking credit for your work. He's protecting you. Researchers who study workplace behaviour found something fascinating: We assume workplace gossip is always malicious, people tearing each other down behind closed doors. But studies by organizational sociologists found that over seventy percent of everyday office chatter is neutral, and a huge chunk is what scientists call prosocial gossip. People sharing information to protect coworkers and enforce fairness. Kevin wasn't gossiping to hurt anyone. He was watching your back.
