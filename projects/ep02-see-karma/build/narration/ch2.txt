On day three, a man at the mall hands you a free sample. He's smiling, but his number doesn't move. Not even by one. Because karma, you realize, can tell the difference between a gift and a hook. And the hook works. In a famous experiment, a researcher bought people a can of Coke as a small, unexpected favor. Later, he asked them to buy raffle tickets. The people who got the Coke bought about twice as many tickets. Even the ones who didn't like him. A small favor makes us feel like we owe something back. It's called reciprocity. It's one of the strongest forces in human behaviour, and salespeople know it. Ever wondered why supermarkets hand out free samples? Now you know. You buy the thing anyway. Your number drops by one, for buying something you didn't need with money you'd promised to save.

So on day four, you come up with a plan. You're going to farm karma. You help an old man carry his groceries. You hold doors. You tell everyone their haircut looks great. Your number goes up. Plus seventy-four. Plus seventy-five. Slowly. Painfully slowly. By evening, you've helped eleven people, your feet hurt, and you've gained four points. It's almost like the number knows when kindness is a performance. Then, on the way home, you buy two hot chocolates, and for no reason at all, you hand one to a stranger shivering at the bus stop. She looks at you like you just handed her the moon. Plus six. In one second. Psychologists actually tested this. They had people give hot chocolate to strangers in a park, then asked them to guess how the strangers would feel. The givers guessed wrong. Every time. The strangers felt much better than the givers predicted. We constantly underestimate how much a small kindness means to someone else.
