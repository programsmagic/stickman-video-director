On day five, you see her again. The woman from the bus stop. She's at the hospital entrance, holding the door for a man on crutches. The man on crutches, ten minutes later, lets a mother with a crying baby go ahead of him in line. And the mother, on her way out, pays for the coffee of the person behind her. One hot chocolate. Three more good things you never would have known about. And none of them will ever know it started with you. Scientists have found something like this in experiments. When people played games where they could share with a group, generosity spread from person to person, up to three steps away. Each extra bit of sharing ended up multiplied, as others copied it. But here's the uncomfortable part. Selfishness spread just as easily. Every day, you're starting a chain reaction. You just never get to see where it ends. The only choice is which kind.

Then, on day six, something changes. Everyone can see everyone's karma. And the world goes a little crazy. People start doing good deeds for the camera. Restaurants only let in high numbers. Dating apps sort by karma. Within hours, someone builds an app that ranks everyone in the city, live. That evening, Leo calls you. His number dropped after he snapped at a waiter, and now strangers are staring at him. He sounds scared. "What if people only ever see the worst thing I did?" And people with low numbers get treated like criminals. Then something strange happens. A man in your building loses his job. And the neighbours whisper, "He must have deserved it." But you can see his number. Plus one thousand, nine hundred and fifty. Psychologists call this the just-world belief. We want to believe the world is fair, that good things happen to good people and bad things happen to bad ones. So when something bad happens to someone, we quietly assume they earned it. It makes us feel safe. And it makes us cruel. Sometimes, bad things happen to people with great numbers.
