## Script

### 1 · HOOK
**[Midnight. Mirror. A number flickers above Magi's head: "+73".]**
You wake up, look in the mirror, and there's a number above your head again.
But this time, it isn't money.
Plus seventy-three.
It's your karma. Every kind thing you've ever done, minus every cruel one, added into a single score.
**[Out the window: numbers over every head. "+410". "-38". "+2,300". "-1".]**
Everyone has one.
For the next seven days, you can see exactly how good, or how bad, everyone really is.
And by day seven, the lowest number in the city will belong to someone you'd never expect.
**[Freeze on a red "-???" above a hidden figure. Scene text: "7 DAYS. EVERY KARMA."]**

### 2 · DAY 1 — THE STREET
At first, it's just fascinating.
**[Barista: "+410". Man shouting into his phone: "-38".]**
The barista who remembers everyone's name is at plus four hundred and ten.
The man yelling at his phone is at minus thirty-eight.
Fair enough.
But then you see the man in the expensive suit. The one who looks like he'd step over you to catch a taxi.
**[Suit: "+2,300".]**
Plus two thousand, three hundred.
You don't know what he did. You'll never know. But he did a lot of it.
And the little girl holding her mum's hand? Plus ninety. Already beating you.
**[Small girl with a balloon: "+90". Magi looks at his own "+73".]**
Lesson one, before breakfast: just like money, you can't see goodness from the outside.
**[Scene text: "YOU CAN'T SEE GOODNESS FROM THE OUTSIDE."]**

### 3 · DAY 2 — KEVIN
Then you walk into work.
And there's Kevin.
Kevin, who forwards your emails and calls it teamwork.
**[Kevin at his desk: "+880".]**
Kevin is at plus eight hundred and eighty.
That's more than ten times your number.
You can't stop staring, so at lunch, you ask around.
Kevin covers night shifts for a coworker whose kid is sick. Every week. He never mentioned it.
**[Flashback doodle: Kevin at a dark office, coffee, clock at 2 a.m.]**
You ask him why he never told anyone.
He shrugs. "Didn't seem like the kind of thing you announce."
You've spent years deciding who Kevin was from the tiny part of him you could see.
You were wrong about his salary last week.
You were wrong about this, too.

### 4 · DAY 3 — THE FREE GIFT
On day three, a man at the mall hands you a free sample.
**[Kiosk: smiling salesman holding a tiny cup. His number: "+0".]**
He's smiling, but his number doesn't move. Not even by one.
Because karma, you realize, can tell the difference between a gift and a hook.
And the hook works.
In a famous experiment, a researcher bought people a can of Coke as a small, unexpected favor. Later, he asked them to buy raffle tickets.
**[Coke can doodle; two stacks of tickets, one twice as tall.]**
The people who got the Coke bought about twice as many tickets. Even the ones who didn't like him.
A small favor makes us feel like we owe something back.
It's called reciprocity. It's one of the strongest forces in human behaviour, and salespeople know it.
Ever wondered why supermarkets hand out free samples? Now you know.
You buy the thing anyway. Your number drops by one, for buying something you didn't need with money you'd promised to save.
**[Magi's "+73" flips to "+72". Small "tap".]**

### 5 · DAY 4 — FARMING KARMA
So on day four, you come up with a plan.
You're going to farm karma.
**[Magi in a cape made of a towel, flexing.]**
You help an old man carry his groceries. You hold doors. You tell everyone their haircut looks great.
**[Montage: groceries, doors, a thumbs-up.]**
Your number goes up. Plus seventy-four. Plus seventy-five.
Slowly. Painfully slowly.
By evening, you've helped eleven people, your feet hurt, and you've gained four points.
It's almost like the number knows when kindness is a performance.
Then, on the way home, you buy two hot chocolates, and for no reason at all, you hand one to a stranger shivering at the bus stop.
**[Bus stop. A woman in a thin coat. Magi holds out a steaming cup.]**
She looks at you like you just handed her the moon.
**[Her face lights up. Hearts pop. Magi's number: "+81".]**
Plus six. In one second.
Psychologists actually tested this. They had people give hot chocolate to strangers in a park, then asked them to guess how the strangers would feel.
The givers guessed wrong. Every time. The strangers felt much better than the givers predicted.
We constantly underestimate how much a small kindness means to someone else.

### 6 · DAY 5 — THE RIPPLE
On day five, you see her again.
The woman from the bus stop.
She's at the hospital entrance, holding the door for a man on crutches.
**[Hospital door. Woman, man on crutches. Her number ticks up.]**
The man on crutches, ten minutes later, lets a mother with a crying baby go ahead of him in line.
**[Queue. Man gestures "after you". Mother smiles. Numbers tick up across the scene.]**
And the mother, on her way out, pays for the coffee of the person behind her.
One hot chocolate. Three more good things you never would have known about.
And none of them will ever know it started with you.
**[Chain doodle: cup → door → line → coffee, connected by arrows.]**
Scientists have found something like this in experiments.
When people played games where they could share with a group, generosity spread from person to person, up to three steps away. Each extra bit of sharing ended up multiplied, as others copied it.
But here's the uncomfortable part.
Selfishness spread just as easily.
**[Same chain, in red: a man cuts the line; the next person shoves; a third snaps at the cashier.]**
Every day, you're starting a chain reaction. You just never get to see where it ends.
The only choice is which kind.

### 7 · DAY 6 — EVERYONE CAN SEE
Then, on day six, something changes.
**[TV: "BREAKING".]**
Everyone can see everyone's karma.
And the world goes a little crazy.
**[Montage: people filming themselves helping; a "TOP KARMA" leaderboard; a shop sign "+500 ONLY".]**
People start doing good deeds for the camera. Restaurants only let in high numbers. Dating apps sort by karma.
Within hours, someone builds an app that ranks everyone in the city, live.
That evening, Leo calls you. His number dropped after he snapped at a waiter, and now strangers are staring at him.
He sounds scared. "What if people only ever see the worst thing I did?"
**[Leo on the phone, sunglasses off, number "-14" in red.]**
And people with low numbers get treated like criminals.
Then something strange happens.
A man in your building loses his job.
**[Man carrying a box out of an office. Neighbours whispering.]**
And the neighbours whisper, "He must have deserved it."
But you can see his number.
**[His number: "+1,950".]**
Plus one thousand, nine hundred and fifty.
Psychologists call this the just-world belief. We want to believe the world is fair, that good things happen to good people and bad things happen to bad ones.
So when something bad happens to someone, we quietly assume they earned it.
**[Two doodles: a scale tipping; a finger pointing at the man.]**
It makes us feel safe. And it makes us cruel.
Sometimes, bad things happen to people with great numbers.

### 8 · DAY 7 — THE LOWEST NUMBER
Remember the lowest number in the city? The one you were never supposed to expect?
**[Back to the hidden "-???".]**
On day seven, you finally find it.
It's above a woman in a grey uniform, standing next to a parking meter.
**[Parking officer writing a ticket: "-4,120".]**
Minus four thousand, one hundred and twenty.
She's a parking officer. Every day, people curse at her, slam their car doors, and wish her the worst.
But here's the thing.
Her number isn't low because of what she's done.
It's low because of what everyone else has said to her.
**[Text: "HER NUMBER ISN'T HER."]**
And when you look closer, the number flickers. Underneath, faint and small, there's a second one.
**[Faint "+3,600" under the red number.]**
Plus three thousand, six hundred. Her real karma.
The big red number was never hers. It was ours.
You walk over, and you tell her she's doing a hard job well.
**[She looks up, surprised. A small smile.]**
She laughs. She says nobody has said that to her in eleven years.
Then she tells you about a man who once thanked her for a ticket, because it reminded him to fix his brakes before a long drive with his kids.
She still remembers his face.

### 9 · ENDING
At midnight, the numbers fade.
**[Street at night, numbers dissolving.]**
The last one you see is your own.
**[Mirror: "+81".]**
Plus eighty-one. Not huge. Not perfect. But higher than a week ago.
And you realize what moved it most wasn't the grand gestures.
It was a hot chocolate. A text to Leo asking if he was okay. One kind sentence to a woman everyone else yells at.
So before bed, you send one more message. A thank-you to your mum, for something she did fifteen years ago that you never mentioned.
**[Phone doodle: "Thank you, Mum." A heart reply.]**
Then Kevin texts you a photo. He's covering another night shift, and the office coffee machine is broken again.
You laugh, grab your coat, and bring him a coffee.
**[Night office: Magi hands Kevin a cup. Kevin grins.]**
Some numbers you'll never see go up. Some kindness nobody will ever count.
That's fine. It still counts.
There's a famous study that found people who spent money on others felt happier than people who spent it on themselves. A later attempt to repeat it didn't find the same effect, so scientists are still arguing about it.
But you don't need a study for this one.
Here's what seven days of seeing everyone's karma taught you.
You can't see goodness from the outside.
Small kindness travels further than you think.
Bad luck doesn't mean a bad person.
And sometimes, the lowest number is just the one everyone else wrote.
**[Scene text: "BE THE GOOD PART OF SOMEONE'S DAY."]**

### 10 · END CARD
Tomorrow, the numbers are gone.
But you wake up hearing whispers.
Everything everyone says about you, when you're not in the room.
**[Mirror: Magi with a hand to his ear. Scene text: "NEXT: WHAT IF YOU COULD HEAR WHAT EVERYONE SAYS BEHIND YOUR BACK?"]**
If you want to hear it too, subscribe. Because tomorrow, the whispers begin.
