## Script

### 1 · HOOK (0:00–0:30)
**[Alarm clock rings. Stickman sits up, rubs eyes.]**
You wake up, look in the mirror, and there's a number floating above your head.
**[Mirror: "$41,200" hovering above his reflection. He swats at it. It doesn't move.]**
Forty-one thousand, two hundred dollars.
Your exact income this year. Every dollar, from every source.
You look out the window.
**[Street full of stick figures, each with a number. "$18,000." "$96,500." "$310,000."]**
Everyone has one.
For the next seven days, you can see what every single person earns.
And by day seven, one of those numbers is going to ruin your life.
**[Freeze on a number above someone we can't see yet, blurred. Scene text: "7 DAYS. EVERY SALARY."]**

### 2 · DAY 1 — THE STREET
At first, it's just funny.
**[Barista hands coffee: "$29,000". Man in a suit behind him: "$34,000". Stickman raises an eyebrow.]**
The guy in the expensive suit earns barely more than the barista.
The lady in sweatpants walking her dog?
**[Number scrolls up: "$1,250,000".]**
Over a million.
Lesson one, before you've even had breakfast: you cannot tell what people earn by looking at them.
**[Scene text: "CLOTHES LIE. NUMBERS DON'T."]**
It gets stranger.
**[Teenager on a skateboard rolls past: "$340,000". He's filming himself on his phone.]**
The kid on the skateboard makes more than your whole office floor. He posts videos about skateboarding.
**[Across the street, a woman in scrubs walking home from a night shift: "$71,000". She yawns.]**
The nurse who just worked twelve hours saving lives makes a fifth of that.
You stand on the corner for a long time, just watching the numbers go by.
Because for the first time, you're seeing something nobody is supposed to see:
what the world actually pays for.
**[Wide shot: dozens of numbers floating over a busy street. Stickman tiny in the middle.]**

### 3 · DAY 2 — THE OFFICE
Then you walk into work.
**[Open office. Stickman at his desk, "$41,200". Next desk: Kevin, feet up, phone in hand: "$58,000".]**
Kevin. Kevin who forwards your emails and calls it teamwork.
Kevin makes almost seventeen thousand dollars more than you.
**[Stickman's pen snaps. Small "crack" sound.]**
And here's the uncomfortable part. In most offices, nobody talks about pay, so gaps like this can hide for years.
That's exactly why a growing number of places now require salary ranges in job ads. Several U.S. states do it, and the European Union passed a pay transparency law that pushes companies to explain pay gaps.
**[Map doodle: a few US states and the EU shaded green.]**
The secret salary is slowly dying. You're just seeing it early.

### 4 · DAY 3 — THE RAISE
So you do something you've never done before.
**[Stickman knocks on the boss's door, holding a folder.]**
You ask for a raise.
And this time, you have an unfair advantage.
**[Boss's head: "$212,000". On his desk, a document with a hovering "$55,000 budget".]**
You can see the budget. You know what the role is worth.
You don't guess. You don't apologize.
You say a number.
**[Close-up: boss blinks, sweats, slowly nods.]**
"…Fifty-five?"
**[Stickman's own number flips: $41,200 → $55,000. Soft "chime".]**
Negotiation researchers have found that many people never ask for more at all, and over a whole career, that silence can cost a fortune. You didn't need superpowers to ask. You just needed to know you were allowed to.

### 4b · DAY 3, LATER — THE FRIEND WITH THE CAR
After work, your best friend Leo picks you up.
**[Shiny red sports car pulls up. Leo, sunglasses, grinning. Above his head: "$52,000".]**
Leo has the new car. The watch. The weekend trips. The photos.
And a number barely higher than yours.
**[Doodle: a credit card stretching like a rubber band, about to snap.]**
For years you've felt like you were falling behind Leo.
Now you know Leo is falling too. Just more quietly.
Researchers studying lottery winners in Canada found something wild about this. The bigger a neighbour's lottery win, the more the people living next door borrowed, and the more of them went bankrupt.
**[Street doodle: one house with a new boat; the houses next to it adding cars, pools, TVs… then cracking.]**
Not because they won anything. Because they tried to keep up.
**[Leo honks. Stickman gets in, glances at the dashboard, says nothing.]**
You decide two things. You'll stop comparing yourself to Leo's photos.
And you'll ask him if he's okay.

### 5 · DAY 4 — THE DATE
That night, you have a date.
**[Restaurant. Candle. Across from him: Maya, smiling. Above her head: "$186,000".]**
Maya earns more than three times what you do.
And for a second, something ugly crawls into your chest.
**[Stickman's smile shrinks. A tiny storm cloud doodle above him.]**
Psychologists have a name for this. Social comparison. We don't judge our lives by how much we have. We judge them by how much we have compared to the people around us.
**[Two stick figures on a seesaw, one high, one low.]**
Maya didn't change. Her number just appeared.
**[Maya laughs at his joke. The storm cloud pops.]**
You decide not to let a number sit at the table.
She pays for dessert. You let her.
On the walk to the station, Maya tells you she grew up sharing one bedroom with three sisters.
That big number above her head? She built it one late night at a time.
**[Doodle: a tiny bedroom with four bunk beds, a desk lamp glowing.]**
You realize you were jealous of the number. You never once thought about the work behind it.
Walking home, you ask yourself the big question.
Would you be happier with her number?
Scientists have argued about this for years. One famous study suggested happiness stops rising above a certain income. Newer research found that for most people, happiness keeps rising as income grows, just more slowly. Each extra dollar matters less than the one before.
**[Graph doodle: a line climbing steeply, then bending into a gentle slope. A tiny stickman climbs it, panting.]**
More money helps. But the climb gets steeper, and the view changes less than you'd expect.

### 6 · DAY 5 — HOME
On day five, you visit your parents.
**[Small kitchen. Dad at the stove: "$37,000".]**
Your dad's number is lower than yours.
Lower than yours was before the raise.
**[Stickman stares. Flashback doodles: bike with a bow, school shoes, a laptop with a ribbon.]**
The bike. The school trips. The laptop for college.
All of it, from that number.
**[Dad turns around, smiles, offers a spoon to taste.]**
You realize a salary tells you what someone earns.
It tells you nothing about what they've given.
Later, you ask your dad if he ever wanted more money.
He laughs.
"Of course I did. But I wanted to be home for dinner more."
**[Dad pulls out a chair at the table for him.]**
After dinner, you find your mum doing the bills at the kitchen table.
**[Mum with a calculator, a neat notebook, receipts in piles: "$24,000".]**
Every column balanced. Every bill paid. For thirty years.
She's never earned much.
But nobody in this house has ever gone without.
You sit down next to her, and for once, you ask her to teach you how she does it.
**[Two stick figures leaning over the notebook. A small "paper" sound as a page turns.]**

### 7 · DAY 6 — EVERYONE CAN SEE
Then, on day six, something changes.
**[News doodle: "BREAKING: EVERYONE CAN SEE SALARIES".]**
It isn't just you anymore. Everyone can see everyone's number.
And the world goes a little crazy.
**[Montage: two neighbours arguing over a fence; a man hiding his number under a hat; a shop raising prices for a "$300K" customer.]**
Landlords start raising rent for tenants with big numbers.
Charities only knock on the richest doors.
And for the first time, every boss has to walk past employees who can see exactly how big the gap is.
**[Boss walking down a corridor, employees' eyes following the "$212,000" above his head.]**
At your office, there's a line outside HR that wraps around the building.
**[Long queue of stick figures holding folders, tapping feet.]**
Even Kevin is in it.
You finally ask him how he makes more than you.
He looks embarrassed.
"When they offered me the job, I just asked for more. They said yes."
**[Flashback doodle: young Kevin in an interview, holding up a slightly bigger number on a card.]**
That's it. One sentence, years ago.
That's the thing about salaries. A small difference at the start follows you for years, because every raise is built on top of the number you began with.
**[Two staircases side by side, one starting one step higher, the gap staying the same all the way up.]**
Here's the wild part. This has actually happened.
In Norway, everyone's income and taxes have been public records for a long time. And in 2001, those records went online, searchable by anyone, in seconds.
**[Doodle: laptop with a Norwegian flag and a search bar.]**
Economists studied what happened next. When everyone could suddenly look up everyone else's income, the happiness gap between richer and poorer people grew.
**[Two thermometers: rich one rises, poor one dips.]**
Seeing other people's numbers didn't make people richer. For a lot of them, it just made them feel poorer.
Years later, Norway made searches traceable, so people could see who had looked them up. Searches fell from around sixteen million a year to about two million.
**[Search bar now shows a tiny eye icon. Fewer search bubbles.]**

### 8 · DAY 7 — THE NUMBER THAT RUINS YOUR LIFE
Remember the number from the first morning? The one that was going to ruin your life?
**[Back to the blurred number from the hook. It slowly sharpens.]**
On day seven, you finally see it.
It's above the quiet janitor in your building. The one who's worked there for thirty years.
**[Janitor sweeping, humming: "$9,800,000".]**
Nine point eight million.
**[Stickman drops his coffee. Splash.]**
He bought the building's shares in the eighties. Saved every month. Never told anyone. Today, the dividends alone pay him more than everyone in the office combined.
He waves at you. Same as always.
**[Janitor waves. Stickman slowly waves back.]**
At lunch, you sit next to him on the loading dock.
**[Two figures on a step, sandwiches, pigeons nearby.]**
You ask the only question that matters. Why are you still here?
He shrugs.
"I like the work. I like the people. And I never needed anyone to know my number."
You ask him if he ever felt like giving up on saving.
"Every month for thirty years, I put a little away. Most months, it felt like nothing.
Then one day, I looked up, and it was everything."
**[Small coin doodles stacking into a tall tower beside him.]**
Then he gives you the only financial advice he's ever given anyone.
"Spend less than you make. Own things that pay you. And stop looking at other people's numbers."
**[Scene text: "SPEND LESS. OWN MORE. STOP COMPARING."]**
It didn't ruin your life.
It ruined the way you used to judge people.

### 9 · ENDING — WHAT THE NUMBERS TAUGHT YOU
At midnight, the numbers fade.
**[Street at night, numbers dissolving like smoke.]**
The last one to go is your own.
**[Mirror again. "$55,000" flickers above his reflection.]**
A week ago, that number felt like a grade. Like a score of how much you were worth.
Now it just looks like what it is.
A price someone agreed to pay for your time this year.
It says nothing about your dad's cooking, your mum's notebook, or the janitor who waves every morning.
**[The number flickers once more, then vanishes. Stickman exhales.]**
Here's what seven days of seeing everyone's salary taught you.
You can't read wealth from clothes.
Not knowing what others earn can cost you real money, so ask.
Comparing numbers can steal your happiness faster than any bill.
And the number above someone's head is the least interesting thing about them.
**[Stickman at his desk, number gone, smiling. Scene text: "ASK. DON'T COMPARE."]**

### 10 · END CARD (≈10 s)
Tomorrow, you wake up and see a different number.
**[Morning mirror again. A new glowing number above his head: "+73".]**
Not money.
Karma.
**[Scene text: "NEXT: WHAT IF YOU COULD SEE EVERYONE'S KARMA?"]**
If you want to see what happens next, subscribe. Because tomorrow at midnight, the numbers change again.
**[Subscribe button doodle; stickman taps it. Hold for YouTube end screen.]**
