On day five, you visit your parents. Your dad's number is lower than yours. Lower than yours was before the raise. The bike. The school trips. The laptop for college. All of it, from that number. You realize a salary tells you what someone earns. It tells you nothing about what they've given. Later, you ask your dad if he ever wanted more money. He laughs. "Of course I did. But I wanted to be home for dinner more." After dinner, you find your mum doing the bills at the kitchen table. Every column balanced. Every bill paid. For thirty years. She's never earned much. But nobody in this house has ever gone without. You sit down next to her, and for once, you ask her to teach you how she does it.

Then, on day six, something changes. It isn't just you anymore. Everyone can see everyone's number. And the world goes a little crazy. Landlords start raising rent for tenants with big numbers. Charities only knock on the richest doors. And for the first time, every boss has to walk past employees who can see exactly how big the gap is. At your office, there's a line outside HR that wraps around the building. Even Kevin is in it. You finally ask him how he makes more than you. He looks embarrassed. "When they offered me the job, I just asked for more. They said yes." That's it. One sentence, years ago. That's the thing about salaries. A small difference at the start follows you for years, because every raise is built on top of the number you began with. Here's the wild part. This has actually happened. In Norway, everyone's income and taxes have been public records for a long time. And in 2001, those records went online, searchable by anyone, in seconds. Economists studied what happened next. When everyone could suddenly look up everyone else's income, the happiness gap between richer and poorer people grew. Seeing other people's numbers didn't make people richer. For a lot of them, it just made them feel poorer. Years later, Norway made searches traceable, so people could see who had looked them up. Searches fell from around sixteen million a year to about two million.
