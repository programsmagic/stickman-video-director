You wake up, look in the mirror, and there's a number floating above your head. Forty-one thousand, two hundred dollars. Your exact income this year. Every dollar, from every source. You look out the window. Everyone has one. For the next seven days, you can see what every single person earns. And by day seven, one of those numbers is going to ruin your life.

At first, it's just funny. The guy in the expensive suit earns barely more than the barista. The lady in sweatpants walking her dog? Over a million. Lesson one, before you've even had breakfast: you cannot tell what people earn by looking at them. It gets stranger. The kid on the skateboard makes more than your whole office floor. He posts videos about skateboarding. The nurse who just worked twelve hours saving lives makes a fifth of that. You stand on the corner for a long time, just watching the numbers go by. Because for the first time, you're seeing something nobody is supposed to see: what the world actually pays for.

Then you walk into work. Kevin. Kevin who forwards your emails and calls it teamwork. Kevin makes almost seventeen thousand dollars more than you. And here's the uncomfortable part. In most offices, nobody talks about pay, so gaps like this can hide for years. That's exactly why a growing number of places now require salary ranges in job ads. Several U.S. states do it, and the European Union passed a pay transparency law that pushes companies to explain pay gaps. The secret salary is slowly dying. You're just seeing it early.

So you do something you've never done before. You ask for a raise. And this time, you have an unfair advantage. You can see the budget. You know what the role is worth. You don't guess. You don't apologize. You say a number. "…Fifty-five?" Negotiation researchers have found that many people never ask for more at all, and over a whole career, that silence can cost a fortune. You didn't need superpowers to ask. You just needed to know you were allowed to.
