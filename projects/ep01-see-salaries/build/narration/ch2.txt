After work, your best friend Leo picks you up. Leo has the new car. The watch. The weekend trips. The photos. And a number barely higher than yours. For years you've felt like you were falling behind Leo. Now you know Leo is falling too. Just more quietly. Researchers studying lottery winners in Canada found something wild about this. The bigger a neighbour's lottery win, the more the people living next door borrowed, and the more of them went bankrupt. Not because they won anything. Because they tried to keep up. You decide two things. You'll stop comparing yourself to Leo's photos. And you'll ask him if he's okay.

That night, you have a date. Maya earns more than three times what you do. And for a second, something ugly crawls into your chest. Psychologists have a name for this. Social comparison. We don't judge our lives by how much we have. We judge them by how much we have compared to the people around us. Maya didn't change. Her number just appeared. You decide not to let a number sit at the table. She pays for dessert. You let her. On the walk to the station, Maya tells you she grew up sharing one bedroom with three sisters. That big number above her head? She built it one late night at a time. You realize you were jealous of the number. You never once thought about the work behind it. Walking home, you ask yourself the big question. Would you be happier with her number? Scientists have argued about this for years. One famous study suggested happiness stops rising above a certain income. Newer research found that for most people, happiness keeps rising as income grows, just more slowly. Each extra dollar matters less than the one before. More money helps. But the climb gets steeper, and the view changes less than you'd expect.
